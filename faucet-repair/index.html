When a faucet problem cannot wait

A dripping faucet is a slow waste. A faucet that will not shut off, or a supply line spraying under the sink, is an emergency. Close the shutoff valves under the sink, or the main valve if they will not turn, and call the 24/7 line. We book same-day visits when a technician is free.

Empty the cabinet under the sink so the plumber can reach the valves, and put a bowl under any slow drip.

What the leak location tells you

Where the water shows up points to the failing part.

Drip from the spout

A worn cartridge, washer or seat. Parts-level repair for most models.

Water at the base of the handle

Worn O-rings or a loose packing nut.

Pool inside the cabinet

Supply lines, shutoff valves or the drain connection.

Low flow from the spout

A clogged aerator or a stuck cartridge, often from mineral scale.

Mineral scale and faucets in Santee

Padre Dam Municipal Water District's 2023 report lists hardness of roughly 9 to 18 grains per gallon depending on the plant. The mineral crust that forms at the spout tip and inside cartridges is a typical result, and it stiffens handles and cuts flow.

Unscrewing the aerator and soaking it in vinegar clears a minor blockage. If the handle is stiff or the faucet still drips, a cartridge swap or a replacement faucet is the next move. Quarter-turn shutoff valves, which are cheap to replace, also seize in hard water.

Choosing a replacement faucet

When a faucet is past repair, pick one that fits the sink holes, the supply lines and the way you use it. A single-handle kitchen faucet with a pull-down sprayer suits most kitchens. For a bathroom, check the number of holes in the sink and the spread between handles before you buy.

Brass-bodied faucets with ceramic cartridges tend to outlast plastic ones, and a common brand makes replacement parts easy to find. We install a faucet you supply or help you pick one, and we replace the shutoff valves and supply lines at the same time so the new fixture is not connected to old, seized parts.

Standards and the fixtures you buy

Faucet parts that touch drinking water should carry NSF/ANSI 61 certification, which covers materials in contact with potable water. Lead limits for plumbing fixtures are set by federal rules, so a quality replacement cartridge or faucet will say it is lead-free on the package.

The California Plumbing Code, based on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC), also sets flow limits for new faucets. Ask for a model that meets them, since an older high-flow faucet may not be sold or installed as new.
