When a business plumbing problem cannot wait

A blocked restroom or kitchen line can shut a business for the day. Call the 24/7 line and describe what is happening. We book same-day visits when a technician is free, and after-hours calls are handled like any other.

While you wait, close the affected area to customers and stop using fixtures on that line. Note which drains and fixtures are involved so the plumber can start with the right tools.

Keeping a business plumbing system healthy

Scheduled maintenance costs less than emergency calls for most businesses. A restaurant benefits from regular grease trap service and jetting of kitchen lines. A retail tenant benefits from a camera look at shared lines before a problem becomes a closure.

Know where your main shutoff, cleanouts and floor sinks are, and make sure staff can find them. Post the emergency number where closing crews can see it. A blocked restroom at closing time becomes a morning problem unless someone calls the 24/7 line the same night.

Planning service around business hours

A plumbing failure in a shop, office or restaurant costs more than the repair. Staff cannot work, customers leave and food or stock may be at risk. Ask the plumber how after-hours work is scheduled, so the repair happens when the space is empty and the doors can open on time.

For recurring needs such as grease traps, backflow tests and drain maintenance, put a calendar in place. A short schedule shared with your property manager avoids surprise stoppages and keeps records ready for any inspector who asks.

Information that speeds up a commercial visit

Have the shutoff locations, the age of the building and the layout of any past repairs ready. A tenant should also know who holds the lease obligations for plumbing, because owners and tenants split responsibility in different ways.

Photos of the problem, the fixture list and any earlier inspection notes help the plumber arrive with the right tools. It shortens the first visit and lowers the chance of a second trip.

Standards that apply to business plumbing

Commercial work follows the California Plumbing Code, which is based on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC). That code addresses grease interceptors for kitchens that discharge fats and oils, backflow protection where a building could contaminate the public supply and the number and type of fixtures a space needs.

Water suppliers run their own cross-connection programs, and a backflow device usually needs periodic testing by a certified tester. Ask your plumber which of these apply to your use of the space, and keep the test reports on file.
